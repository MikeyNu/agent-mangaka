# Drawing environmental impact

Use this when the reader needs to judge how a place changed. Start with the actual setting, scripted outcome and delivery format. A close-up of a chipped tile and an aerial view of a missing district answer different questions even if both fill a panel.

## Establish the extent

Draw the original surface lightly. Locate the changed area, its outer boundary, an intact feature and a familiar measure. Write the intended read in everyday units from the scene, such as “one tread chipped beside the boot” or “the gap spans the valley between two known ridges.” A close-up can magnify a small event. Pull back or use a second view when the extent lies outside the crop. If a glowing silhouette hides the ground, leave the damage undetermined until a later drawing reveals it.

| Intended extent | Useful view | Drawing decision |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Local chip, split or dent | Close three-quarter view with a seam, floorboard or foot in the same plane | Keep most of the surface intact. Show one newly exposed edge and its thickness. |
| Floor, wall or room | Oblique view with an opening, paving run or threshold | Carry regular joints up to the interruption. Turn a few slabs to show their undersides. Keep the far end of the damage visible. |
| Street, settlement or landscape | High wide view or low view across a known route | Compare the footprint against roof rows, tree groups, a road or distinct terrain layers. Carry one landmark from a prior view into the aftermath. |
| Vast hollow or distant event | Establishing view, optionally followed by a quiet reverse | Repeat a recognizable measure at two depths. Let distant detail merge and keep landing places, rims or the horizon readable. An empty area can convey more extent than a dense cloud. |

## Draw the broken material

1. **Choose the shape of the change.** A local chip removes a corner. A directional passage leaves two aligned breaks and intervening space. An outward upheaval lifts and turns surfaces around one region. A clean absence leaves a readable boundary. Draw the footprint that belongs to the script. A luminous column, a concentration-line fan, a dust ring and a lasting crater are separate choices.
2. **Build a few large pieces from the original surface.** Carry a grout seam, painted edge, wood grain or layer of earth across a separated piece. Show the old face, newly exposed face and the narrow dark shape where two pieces overlap. Give the nearest fragment a readable thickness. Give distant fragments fewer planes and quieter edges. Vary orientation, size and spacing. A few overlapping pieces establish depth better than equally spaced chips.
3. **Choose a material-specific edge.** Draw paving in plates with a smoother outer face and a rougher broken side. Let layered rock split into stepped planes that follow the local strata. Along timber, draw long narrow fibers and a short ragged end at a cross break. For soil, group clods into soft masses with occasional sharp embedded stones. Check the scene reference before choosing. Carry the local surface colour through every piece.
4. **Separate the kinds of pencil mark.** Press into a short dark wedge beneath a lifted lip and taper out along the parting edge. Let a major fissure narrow, turn or disappear behind a fragment. On a fresh face, run a short family of hatches in one plane and turn or stop it at the next edge. For flight, pull a few tapered marks along a fragment's travel and leave the leading silhouette recoverable. Group grit at selected edges and leave clear intervals. Give dust broad, overlapping lobes and broken, soft contours. A drawn flash can use reserved paper or a light local value. Keep resting cracks, descriptive hatching, movement and light distinguishable when the image is small.
5. **Project the effect into the setting.** A circular front on a receding floor becomes a foreshortened arc. Interrupt it where a raised slab passes in front. Two narrow diagonal contacts call for a different composition from an encircling wave. A straight graphic speed line may cross the drawing as a reader cue. A seam, shadow or scratch belongs to its receiving surface.

## Choose time and colour

For a single panel, pick the instant with the clearest evidence. At contact, a chipped rim might be enough. During displacement, leave air between the ground and a lifted piece. Later, a settled piece casts a contact shadow while dust can still veil a far wall. Carry damage, blocked routes and exposed surfaces forward if the story revisits them. Keep successive views and repeated drawings of one event tied to one change of state.

For a page, use two related views when a close contact and its full reach cannot share one crop. In a vertical comic, reveal the near evidence, then use scroll distance and a wider panel to disclose the far limit. Preview at the intended phone width so the landmark and the boundary can be compared. For an animation or storyboard, select the contact drawing, the pose with the clearest displacement, and the settled silhouette. Time the chunks and dust independently. Adapt the useful drawings to the delivery format. The story determines whether the smaller marks deserve their own beat.

Use monochrome studies to plan line pressure, value grouping, overlap and depth. Render in the project's active palette. A white paper gap might become sky visible through a breach, a highlight on one dusty edge, or a quiet patch of coloured ground. Assign it a role before painting. Let shadows share the scene's lighting. Introduce coloured emission only when the scene supplies a luminous source.

## Pass the decisions into production

Put the selected instant in `moment`, the scale comparison and camera in `composition`, and known landmarks in `visible_landmarks`. Put material, boundary, stroke directions, palette and the clear gap to preserve in `visual_constraints`. Record stable damage to the location in its continuity notes and carry the visible facts into subsequent panel packets. Use existing environment identifiers and mapped landmarks.

Read the thumbnail before the caption or SFX. Can a reader point to what changed, its far limit or the deliberate crop, and one clue to size? Hide glow, speed lines and lettering. Check that exposed faces, overlaps or displaced landmarks still convey the intended change. For a scene with no physical damage, check that intact details support that outcome. Inspect the important chip or crack at native size, then compare the next view against the site plan and the intended coloured treatment.
