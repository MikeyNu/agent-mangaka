# Drawing rage in sequential art

Read the line before and after the turn. Identify what the character just learned, whom or what the anger is directed toward, and what the character wants to do or keep themselves from doing. Write that as an acting direction the artist can draw. “She returns the paper squarely to the table, then looks at the person who signed it” gives the hand, the eyes and the cut a purpose.

## Choose the performance

| Performance | Face and body | How to show the turn |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Contained anger | Set the jaw under a nearly closed mouth. Tension can sit in one lower lid or press one mouth corner flat. Pick a small action that stops, or let the hands become deliberately still. | Hold the same angle long enough for the reader to notice what stopped. Preserve the target of the gaze. |
| Anger breaking through restraint | Pull one upper lip tight over the teeth, bunch the cheek beside the nose, or show a brief slip in an otherwise careful gesture. | In a profile, leave a wedge of space in front of the face. It makes a slight lean or a change of address visible. |
| A vocal outburst | Let the jaw rotate open from near the ear. The mouth corners stretch, the cheeks move and the throat joins the action. A forward weight shift or a rigid planted stance gives the voice a body. | Catch one decisive shape of the spoken line or cry. Keep upper teeth attached to the upper jaw and lower teeth to the moving jaw. Preserve the speaker's usual tooth shapes. |
| Anger behind a smile | Pull the mouth wide while the lower lids stay tense and attention remains fixed. An asymmetric corner or a hand gripping the edge of a chair can betray what the mouth conceals. | Let the trigger, the listener's response or the next deliberate action establish what the smile means. Reuse the same face when the smile drops. |

A wide eye, white iris, bead of sweat or open mouth can also read as fear, pain, surprise or exertion. Check what the character attends to. A flinch and a search for an exit tell a different story from returning the gaze and demanding an answer. Fear, grief and anger can coexist. Preserve their order if the script gives one.

## Draw the actor before the effect

Start from the approved head at the same angle. Place the brow ridge, eyes, nose base and jaw hinge. Bring the inner brows down and together where the performance calls for it. Let the lower lids push back against widened eyes or narrow the opening into a steady glare. Choose one strong tension crease at reading size. Wrap its taper over the bridge, cheek or jaw plane. Preserve familiar eye spacing, lips, nose, hairline, skin values, age and distinctive marks through the deformation.

For a medium or wide shot, hide the facial details and read the silhouette. Compare the shoulder on each side, both hands, the torso angle and where the feet carry the weight. A dropped arm ending in a tightly closed hand can carry more held anger than two raised fists. Keep the listener in character as well. One person reducing their gestures can change the entire exchange.

## Ink and cut to the beat

Use a single-eye crop to hold attention when the preceding panel has established the target. A paired profile cut can intensify opposition if each person retains the correct facing direction. A band of shadow can frame an eye while enough of the cheek or hand stays visible for identification. For a shout, gather radial strokes toward the brow or mouth and leave its outline clear. For a held beat, a few vertical hatches or a quiet background can give a nearly motionless face time. Keep unmarked areas beside the densest strokes.

Eye flares, ink-black masks, rising hair and torn backgrounds can serve as expressive shorthand. Give any literal transformation or environmental damage the cause and consequences specified in the story. Keep hand-drawn emphasis on its own layer when possible. Reserve room for established lettering and keep the script's volume, wording and speaker intact. A quiet line in an ordinary balloon can carry the peak.

**Review at reading size.** Hide dialogue, colour accents and effects. Can you tell who is angry, what they are attending to and whether they are holding it in or letting it out? Restore the script and check the transition. If a quiet question reads as a scream, repair the jaw or gesture first. If a scream reads as fear, inspect the gaze, body direction and preceding panel before adding hatch marks. After the peak, choose the residual action the story earns, such as a hand slow to unclench or a witness still watching.
