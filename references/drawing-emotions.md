# Drawing emotion in manhwa

## Direct the change

Start with the instant a character's understanding changes. Mark what they expected, what just happened, who saw it, and what they want the other person to see. Write a drawable verb for the reaction: search a face for recognition, swallow a reply, steady a hand, return a look, or let a held breath go. Direct the listener as carefully as the speaker. In a quiet conversation, the important face can belong to the person who says nothing.

Use the approved neutral head at the same angle to judge exaggeration. Choose one unmistakable deviation, give it one supporting cue elsewhere, then decide how much of the panel it earns. Someone who habitually grins may show hurt by holding the corners up a beat too long. Someone guarded may show great joy by finally meeting another person's eyes. Three stages make a useful sheet: what the other characters usually see, the first involuntary change, and the fullest display the scene permits. A real sequence can stop at any one of them.

## Choose the emotion the reader needs to understand

These are drawing options. Choose a performance from the story, the character and the size of the beat. The same shapes can take on a different meaning in their next action.

| Emotional beat | When it earns a panel | A drawable choice and a way to distinguish it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Curiosity or concentration | An unfamiliar detail could change what someone does next. | Place the eyes on the specific detail. Let a hand pause, then follow the look with a purposeful reach. A slight parting of the lips can mark a question forming. Concentration can narrow the lids while the body leans *toward* the problem. |
| Confusion or doubt | The answer conflicts with what the character thought they knew. | Give one brow more lift, leave the mouth half formed, and move the gaze between the two pieces of evidence. Suspicion settles on the person who supplied the answer. Confusion may keep searching. |
| Surprise or disbelief | An expectation breaks before its meaning is clear. | Expose a little more white above the iris, interrupt the hand's task, leave air between the lips. Hold that first face briefly. The next look toward a friend, a threat or a familiar object gives the surprise its direction. |
| Worry, dread or fear | Someone anticipates a cost, senses danger, or sees a threat close in. | Worry can keep returning to one door while the character tries to talk. Dread can quiet the entire body. For an abrupt scare, widen the eyes and draw the shoulders or weight back. For fear held under control, keep the hands working and let the gaze betray it. |
| Shock or numbness | The news outpaces the character's ability to answer. | Keep the mouth slightly open and a once-purposeful gesture unfinished. A nearly unchanged second frame can be more disturbing than a larger grimace. Let movement return when the character has processed some of what happened. |
| Hurt, sadness or grief | A loss becomes real or an ordinary detail recalls someone who is missing. | Lift the inner brows, weight the upper lids, or catch moisture along the lower lid before it spills. Put the gesture in relation to the loss. A hand covering the mouth before tears cross the knuckles reads differently from wiping sweat after a run. Grief can also stay dry-eyed. |
| Disappointment or resignation | A hoped-for answer fails, or a person accepts a limit. | Bring a raised chin or expectant hand down. Let the eyes linger on the failed outcome before turning away. Resignation can settle into stillness while disappointment may still look back for a different answer. |
| Irritation, anger or rage | A boundary has been crossed, an injustice is recognized, or restraint gives way. | Tighten the mouth or lower the brow over an anchored gaze. The trigger and the person addressed distinguish anger from exertion. Let the quieter character hold a precise gesture. See [Drawing rage](drawing-rage.md) for held anger, a smile under pressure and vocal outbursts. |
| Disgust or contempt | The character rejects something they see or hear. | For disgust, shorten the nose-to-upper-lip distance and pull away from the offending thing. For contempt, a single mouth corner or a deliberate glance away can dismiss a person. Use the object or exchange to establish which reading fits. |
| Embarrassment, shame or remorse | Someone has been seen unexpectedly, harmed someone, or must take responsibility. | Embarrassment can dart a look at a witness and recover with a small laugh. Shame may turn the head away while the shoulders close. For remorse, let the person look back and stay for the answer. A blush by itself establishes none of these. |
| Amusement, joy or pride | A joke lands, company becomes easy, or effort is acknowledged. | Give the smile lift in the cheeks as well as the lips. In laughter, upper teeth belong to the upper jaw and the throat can open with the breath. Pride can be smaller: a glance back at good work, a level chin, a hand that stops fidgeting. Let another person's reaction give the laugh its social meaning. |
| Affection, trust or relief | Someone chooses closeness, recognizes safety, or receives the answer they needed. | Aim a soft gaze at the recipient and use the distance between bodies. Relief needs the earlier tension: release the pinched mouth, lower one shoulder, or let the fingers finally close around someone. Tears after recognition can coexist with joy or loss. |
| Resolve or defiance | A decision replaces hesitation and changes the next action. | Bring the weight over a planted foot. Let the eyes return to a chosen task and the jaw settle. A narrowed eye can resemble anger. Show what the character has decided to do. |
| Fatigue, boredom or composure | A long wait, expenditure of effort, or deliberate social mask matters to the scene. | All can have low lids and a narrow mouth. Fatigue borrows support from a wall or hand. Boredom lets attention drift. Composure tracks the exchange and answers precisely. Use the next movement to resolve the ambiguity. |

## Build the eyes and mouth as a pair

Draw the head turn and eye line first. Anchor inner and outer corners to the skull. Let the upper lid cover or reveal the iris and the lower lid press up with the cheek. Test brow height and tilt independently from lid opening. A lifted brow above a steady, half-lidded eye can be skeptical. Raised inner brows above eyes that keep checking the listener can suggest a need for reassurance. Keep the far eye foreshortened in three-quarter view. A glint follows the light source unless the panel deliberately shifts into a graphic treatment.

Draw the mouth opening as one dark shape before adding teeth. Decide whether it is a narrow held breath, a sideways pull against clenched teeth, an uneven opening around a difficult word, or a broad laugh. Place its corners on the curved front plane of the face and let the lower jaw pivot near the ear. Couple a smile with the cheek rising against the lower lid, or keep the eyes watchful when the smile is performed for somebody. A slight parting can carry surprise, listening, relief or pain. Give it a gaze target and an action. Teeth, lip fullness and resting asymmetry belong to the actor. Keep them through exaggeration and match speech shapes to the actual volume and line.

## Draw the light and the hand of the artist

Block the physical light before choosing expressive emphasis. At thumbnail size place the key direction, the eye socket, the nose's cast shadow and the area readable around the mouth. Turn the head under that key. The shadow can cross the face while the lamp stays in the same place.

| Lighting decision | Use it to reveal | Detail to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Broad front or three-quarter light | Small changes in lids, cheek lift and hesitant mouth corners during conversation. | Keep enough value separation at the lower lip. Preserve the local complexion. |
| Side or raking light | Divide a composed face from a jaw tensing on the shadow side. It can catch a wet lower lid in profile. | Carry the nose shadow in the same direction as the face turns. Leave the decisive feature readable on the dark side. |
| Top light or cast shadow | Hide how much the character gives away until a lifted chin or changed sightline reveals the eyes. | Use the brow, hair or setting to account for the shadow. Across a cut, track which eye becomes visible. |
| Backlight or rim light | Separate bodies at an encounter or hold a silhouette during a silent reaction. | A shoulder dropping or the gap between two hands must survive the loss of facial detail. |
| Underlight, a flat tone or a graphic blackout | Mark an exceptional reveal, private perception or sudden break in attention. | Establish a low source for literal underlight. For a subjective break, give the reader a return to the ordinary scene. A colour wash alone cannot establish what the person feels. |

Use pencil pressure to place the tension. On a trembling lower lip, draw a light contour, lift briefly and pick it up with one short offset stroke that rejoins the form. On a held jaw, press once along the shadow-side angle and taper before the chin. In a cry, let a few quicker strokes overshoot at the outer cheek or collar. Keep the mouth aperture and eye corners structurally clear. For warmth or relief, open the spacing of the marks and give the lit cheek clean paper. For dread, build a close group of parallel hatches at the brow, then let them die out into a quieter temple. When doubt lingers, an interrupted line or one redrawn edge can suit the hesitation. The intention belongs in the placement, speed, pressure and release of the trace. Covering the whole drawing in doubled outlines makes the face harder to read.

Let pleasure disrupt the line too. For a laugh someone tries to suppress, keep the first mouth small and press briefly into the raised cheek. When the laugh escapes, sweep a loose arc through the cheek and collar, add a short bouncing echo at the lifted shoulder, and open the jaw on the breath. For a forced laugh, the mouth can get just as large while the shoulders stay braced and the eyes keep checking the listener. In both versions preserve the same lamp, skin colour and head construction. The contradiction between marks, pose and timing does the acting.

## Stage time and test the read

Establish the trigger and the listener's position. Decide whether the reader should see the involuntary reaction before the mask returns, or discover it through a later gesture. A held camera makes a small change legible. A profile can show a tear crossing a hand. A close crop can isolate the instant a mouth fails to make the next word. A wider view can show a speaker leaning in while the listener gives them space. Use a wider gutter for a meaningful silence and return to a shared landmark when the conversation resumes. Keep lettering out of the decisive eyes, lips and contact between hands.

Test a difficult panel at final reading size in grayscale. First look at the face with the effects hidden. Then cover the mouth and read the eyes and gesture. Cover the eyes and read the mouth and body. Mixed signals can be exactly right when someone masks fear or tries to smile through grief. Read the preceding and following panels to see whether the intended contradiction is intelligible. Repair a misplaced gaze, mouth corner, shadow boundary or one muddy contour before increasing intensity. Compare the actor against their approved sheet again after the repair.
